# Stop Counting, Start Designing: Rethinking Accessibility Metrics

## Article overview

This article challenges the use of broad disability prevalence figures and assistive-technology counts as substitutes for product evidence. It argues that digital teams should measure the barriers their products create, the functional and emotional needs people experience, and the outcomes of design decisions.

The article is not an argument against measurement. Its stronger proposition is: **stop counting what does not guide design, and start measuring what can improve experience**.

This semantic edition also adds an important qualification. Needs-based data cannot always replace disability-demographic data. Organisations may still need voluntary, privacy-conscious demographic information to identify structural exclusion, evaluate equality, meet legal duties or understand whether disabled people receive comparable outcomes. The appropriate model often uses both types of evidence for different purposes.

## The underlying problem

There are deeply flawed assumptions about accessibility, assistive technology and how disabled people are framed in digital design. These assumptions are often well-intentioned, but they can perpetuate misunderstandings and limit meaningful progress.

Rather than looking only for familiar answers, should designers start asking better questions?

## 1. Who are we really designing for?

The question, “How many people with disabilities use our website or application?” is primarily a demographic question about identity, legal status or medical diagnosis. On its own, it is not a sufficient accessibility question.

Some disabled people use assistive technologies to improve access and usability. Others do not, and preferences vary among people with similar conditions. Accessibility therefore cannot be understood simply by counting assistive-technology use or diagnostic categories.

Accessibility design needs evidence about:

- what people are trying to do;
- which barriers prevent or complicate that activity;
- the different ways people perceive, understand and operate the product;
- the adaptations, technologies and strategies they use;
- the context in which the interaction occurs; and
- whether the resulting experience is effective, dignified and comparable.

Many people who do not identify as disabled also use features such as pinch-to-zoom, captions and voice input. These features can support people experiencing temporary impairments or situational limitations. W3C, for example, recognises that accessibility can benefit people using different devices, older people, people with temporary disabilities and people facing situational limitations.

This does not make disability and assistive technology irrelevant or interchangeable. A feature may be convenient for one person and essential for another. Mainstream benefit should strengthen the design case without erasing the rights or experiences of disabled people.

Rather than asking only how many users are disabled, ask:

> **How many people is our site disabling?**

This shifts attention towards barriers introduced by design, where many practical solutions begin.

## 2. ROI is not a dirty word

The article to which this piece originally responded appeared to suggest that requests for accessibility data imply a search for return on investment, and that such a search is ethically suspect.

But why should ROI be excluded from the conversation? Disabled customers are customers.

ROI does not have to mean short-term profit. It may include:

- task completion;
- engagement;
- customer satisfaction;
- retention;
- reach;
- reduced support demand;
- lower remediation costs;
- brand trust; and
- reduced regulatory or litigation exposure.

If accessibility is considered in terms of needs, barriers and usability as well as demographics, organisations can develop more useful evidence while respecting privacy.

However, ROI must not become a gatekeeping test. Equal access is not optional merely because a group is small or a financial return is difficult to quantify. Ethics, rights and law can require action independently of a positive commercial calculation.

### Captions as an example

Captions illustrate how a feature designed to meet disability-related access needs may also support a much wider audience. People may use captions because they are Deaf or hard of hearing, because speech is unclear, because they are learning a language, because the environment is noisy, or because they need to keep the sound low.

The original draft stated that data from Facebook, Twitter, Netflix and the BBC showed that 40% to 80% of users watch with captions enabled. That range should not be treated as a universal statistic. Figures vary by platform, country, age, content type, device, study method and whether the measure is people, sessions or viewing hours. Each figure needs a dated primary source and a clearly defined denominator.

As one current example, Netflix reported in 2025 that nearly half of viewing hours in the United States occurred with subtitles or captions. That finding supports the wider design argument, but it should not be generalised automatically to other platforms or populations.

Caption use can demonstrate engagement and market value. It also remains an essential accessibility requirement for many people, regardless of wider uptake.

## 3. Do not undermine “accessibility benefits everyone”

If organisations claim that accessibility benefits everyone, they should be willing to test the claim. Repeating it without observation or evidence can undermine trust.

Rather than measuring only by condition, teams can also measure by need, barrier and outcome. Aggregated needs-based measures may reveal patterns that diagnostic categories do not. This can help reframe accessibility as a general design principle rather than a niche accommodation.

But “benefits everyone” should be used carefully. Not every accessibility measure benefits every person, and broad usefulness is not the source of disabled people's right to access. The more defensible claim is:

> Accessibility is essential for many disabled people and can also improve experiences for a wider range of people and situations.

## 4. Privacy and data are not an either/or choice

“Privacy trumps metrics” is an oversimplification. Privacy matters, especially where data could reveal medical information, disability identity, stigma or exposure to discrimination. But not every useful accessibility measure requires medical or diagnostic data.

Organisations can often learn from:

- accessibility feature settings where collection is transparent and necessary;
- task success and failure;
- abandonment points;
- error recovery;
- zoom or reflow failures;
- caption and transcript activation;
- alternative-input success;
- support requests;
- reported barriers; and
- voluntary research about functional and emotional experience.

These measures are not automatically anonymous or ethically neutral. A combination of preferences, behaviour and context can reveal or allow inference about disability. Collection therefore needs purpose limitation, data minimisation, security, retention controls, transparent explanation and a lawful basis where applicable.

Users should have meaningful choices where consent or opt-out is appropriate. However, an opt-out does not by itself make a methodology ethical or lawful. Teams must still ask whether the data should be collected at all, who can access it and how it may affect people.

The objective is not to choose between ethics and insight. It is to design an ethical research and measurement method.

## 5. Metrics can reveal market potential

Analytics can help demonstrate market potential. Data about needs, barriers and feature value may be more actionable for product teams than a broad population estimate.

### Apple as an example

Apple provides features addressing vision, hearing, speech, mobility and cognitive access needs. Some of these features also have uses beyond their original disability-related contexts.

It is reasonable to use Apple as an example of accessibility integrated into product design and brand positioning. It is not safe to infer the company's complete commercial, ethical or legal motivation without primary evidence. Corporate decisions can have several rationales at once.

### UK broadcasting as an example

The original draft referred to collaboration involving the Independent Television Commission, BBC and ITV in the development of early audio-description services and audio-led production during the 1990s. It argued that this investment supported blind and vision-impaired audiences, influenced brand identity and contributed time-coded descriptive metadata for audiovisual content.

The history of UK audio description is relevant, but claims about invention, institutional responsibility, dates, “world first” status and subsequent technical uses require verification against broadcasting archives and primary sources. They should not be learned as established history solely from this article.

The wider point remains useful: accessibility investments may be driven by ethics, regulation, public-service duties, customer benefit, innovation and commercial value simultaneously. Each form of return needs different evidence.

## 6. Mainstreaming assistive technology

“If our site is inaccessible to voice-control users, they will go elsewhere.” This may be true in competitive contexts, but the significance of voice interaction is broader than customer choice.

Voice interaction is now present in mainstream products such as Siri and Alexa. Using voice interaction does not establish that a person is disabled. Equally, mainstream use does not stop voice control from being assistive technology or from being essential to some disabled people.

As Scotty discovered in *Star Trek IV*, talking to a computer can become an ordinary interaction model.

The same pattern applies to larger text, dark mode, keyboard operation, text-to-speech and other adaptive features. These may support permanent disabilities, temporary impairments, situational limitations or personal preferences.

Examples of context include:

- tiredness;
- stress;
- glare;
- noisy or quiet environments;
- temporary injury;
- divided attention;
- unavailable audio; and
- constraints created by a device, connection or battery.

Design informed by qualitative and quantitative evidence from people with specific access needs can improve the experience for others as well. However, research should include people for whom access is essential, not only mainstream users who find a feature convenient.

## 7. The intersectionality of needs

The current World Health Organization estimate is that approximately **1.3 billion people, or 16% of the global population**, experience significant disability. The original draft used a range of 15% to 20%.

That global estimate is important for public policy and broad context, but it is not a product-research sample or a model of digital access needs. It cannot tell a design team which barriers exist in a particular journey.

International disability data also varies according to definitions, questions, survey methods, reporting conditions and national data systems. That does not make the data worthless, but it limits simple comparisons and product-level conclusions.

Use prevalence figures for context, strategic planning and diversity reporting where appropriate. For product strategy, also gather evidence about:

- barriers;
- tasks and contexts;
- preferences;
- interaction modes;
- outcomes;
- cumulative and intersectional effects; and
- change over time.

Needs-based research should not erase identity or intersectionality. Disability can interact with age, race, gender, income, language, geography, education and access to technology. Two people with similar functional needs may experience very different levels of exclusion.

## 8. Focus on needs, without discarding demographics

Should accessibility be prioritised regardless of how many disabled users there are?

Morally, this is a strong question. Legally, equal-access duties may also apply regardless of the size of the measured audience. But the question can miss an opportunity: designing for needs can turn accessibility from a compliance exercise into a source of better products and new interaction models.

Instead of measuring identity **or** experience, use each measure for the question it can legitimately answer:

| Evidence type | Questions it can help answer | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Disability-demographic data | Are disabled people represented? Are outcomes comparable? Are particular groups being excluded? | Which design barrier caused an outcome or how to fix it |
| Functional-needs data | Which modes of perception, understanding or operation must the design support? | Whether a protected group experiences systemic disadvantage |
| Behavioural analytics | Where do people fail, abandon, repeat or seek help? | Why the behaviour occurred or whether the person is disabled |
| Qualitative research | Why does a barrier occur? How does it feel? What adaptation works? | Population prevalence or statistical scale |
| Accessibility testing | Which technical or experiential requirements fail? | Complete real-world impact across all people and contexts |
| Customer-experience metrics | Whether experience, trust or satisfaction changes over time | Legal compliance or absence of discrimination |

Accessibility work is driven by three related forces:

1. **Ethics:** building an organisational culture that respects disabled people and removes avoidable barriers.
2. **Law:** meeting applicable equality, accessibility, consumer and data-protection requirements.
3. **Opportunity:** improving products, services and experiences by designing for a wider range of needs and contexts.

To be inclusive, designers and product owners need to understand both the scale and nature of the barriers they create, and the effect of the solutions they introduce. That requires responsible, ethical and useful data.

## A practical measurement model

The article's argument can be converted into four layers of evidence.

### Layer 1: Technical and design evidence

Measure whether designs and implementations meet defined accessibility requirements. Combine automated checks with manual evaluation and review in context.

### Layer 2: Behavioural evidence

Identify where people encounter friction, fail, abandon, repeat actions or use an alternative route. Do not infer disability from these behaviours.

### Layer 3: Experiential evidence

Ask people about functional success, effort, confidence, dignity, satisfaction and emotional experience. Include disabled people with different access needs.

### Layer 4: Equity evidence

Where lawful and proportionate, examine whether disabled people and relevant intersectional groups receive comparable access and outcomes. Keep equality-monitoring data separated from operational decision-making.

Together, these layers answer more useful questions than a single prevalence figure or assistive-technology count.

## Conclusion

Until designers ask better questions about barriers, needs and contexts, rather than relying on demographics alone, they will continue to fall short of inclusive design.

If better data is part of the answer, the starting point is better questions. Data should cover both functional and emotional experience. Teams should track change over time and measure what matters.

Only then can outcomes be demonstrated and improved—and the claim that **accessibility benefits everyone** can become more than a slogan, without losing sight of the people for whom accessibility is essential.
